How to Set Your Freelance Rates and Track Billable Hours

How to set freelance and consulting rates that actually pay the bills, plus how to track billable hours accurately so you stop leaving money on the table and invoice with confidence.

You went freelance—or hung out your shingle as an independent consultant—for the freedom. But two questions keep you up at night. First: Am I charging enough? You picked a number that sounded reasonable, but you have no idea if it covers your actual costs and leaves a real profit. Second: Where did all my billable time go? You worked all week, but when it's time to invoice, you're squinting at your calendar trying to reconstruct what you actually did for whom.

Both problems cost you real money. Underpricing means you work harder for less. Sloppy time tracking means hours you actually worked never make it onto an invoice. This guide fixes both: how to set a rate grounded in your real numbers, and how to track billable hours so every minute you work gets paid.

Why "What Everyone Else Charges" Is a Bad Starting Point

The most common way people set rates is to peek at what other freelancers charge and land somewhere in the middle. That's a trap. Other people's rates reflect their costs, their expenses, and their target income—none of which are yours. Your rate has to start from your numbers and work outward.

The second trap is thinking like an employee. If you made $75,000 as a salaried developer, you might assume an equivalent freelance income is $75,000. It isn't. As a freelancer you pay your own taxes, health insurance, software, equipment, and downtime. You also don't bill 40 hours a week—a huge chunk of your time goes to finding clients, sending proposals, and doing admin. Your rate has to absorb all of that.

Step 1: Calculate Your Target Income

Start with what you actually need to earn in a year to live and run the business. Add up:

  • The take-home income you want
  • Self-employment taxes (set aside roughly 25–30% of profit)
  • Business expenses: software subscriptions, hardware, insurance, accounting, your phone and internet share
  • A buffer for retirement and slow months

Say you want $70,000 take-home. After taxes, expenses, and a modest buffer, your business needs to bring in around $110,000 in revenue. That's your real target—not the $70,000.

Step 2: Figure Out Your Real Billable Hours

Here's the number that surprises everyone. You do not bill 40 hours a week, 52 weeks a year (2,080 hours). Reality:

  • Subtract vacation, holidays, and sick time—say 6 weeks. Now you're at 46 weeks.
  • Of each working week, only a fraction is billable. Marketing, proposals, invoicing, email, and admin eat 30–40% of your time. If you bill 25 hours of a 40-hour week, that's realistic for a solo operator.

So: 46 weeks × 25 billable hours = 1,150 billable hours per year. Not 2,080. This gap is exactly why people who divide their target income by 2,080 end up broke.

Step 3: Do the Math

Now the rate falls out of the numbers:

$110,000 target revenue ÷ 1,150 billable hours = ~$96/hour

That's your floor—the minimum hourly rate that hits your income goal given your real billable capacity. Round it to a clean $95 or $100. If that number feels high compared to what you've been charging, that's the point: you've probably been underpricing because you divided by the wrong number of hours.

For a consultant whose expenses and target are higher, the same math might land at $150 or $200/hour. The formula doesn't change—only your inputs do.

Step 4: Choose a Pricing Model

Your hourly rate is the foundation, but you don't have to bill purely by the hour:

  • Hourly is simple and fair for open-ended or unpredictable work. Track time, bill time.
  • Project / fixed fee works when scope is clear. Estimate the hours, multiply by your rate, add a buffer for revisions, and quote one number. Clients love the predictability—and if you work fast, you keep the upside.
  • Retainer gives you stable monthly income for ongoing work. Estimate monthly hours, multiply by your rate, and bill the same amount each month.

Even when you quote a flat project or retainer fee, you calculate it from your hourly rate and track the hours behind it—so you know whether the deal was actually profitable.

Step 5: Track Every Billable Hour

This is where freelancers quietly bleed money. Work you did but didn't log is work you don't get paid for. To capture all of it:

Track in real time, not from memory. Log time as you work, or right after each task. Reconstructing your week on Friday afternoon guarantees you'll forget the 20-minute calls and quick fixes—and those add up to hundreds of dollars a month.

Record four things per entry: the date, the client/project, what you did, and the duration. That description matters—when a client questions an invoice, "2.5 hrs: debugged checkout API and deployed fix" ends the conversation. "2.5 hrs: work" invites a dispute.

Separate billable from non-billable. Track your admin and marketing time too, even though you don't bill it. Knowing your real billable ratio is what lets you sanity-check your rate over time. If you're only billing 18 hours a week, not 25, your effective income is way below target and your rate needs to rise.

A Worked Example

You spend a week on two clients. You log:

  • Client A: 12.5 billable hours at $100 = $1,250
  • Client B: 8 billable hours at $100 = $800
  • Non-billable: 14 hours (proposals, invoicing, email)

At invoice time you bill $2,050—and every entry has a date, a client, and a clear description, so the invoices write themselves. Without the log, you'd have eyeballed it, undercounted Client A by a couple of hours, and handed back $200 for free. Over a year, that kind of leak is thousands of dollars.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Pricing from desired salary, not real costs. Forgetting taxes, expenses, and downtime is the number-one reason freelancers underprice. Run the full calculation.

Dividing by 2,080 hours. You don't bill full-time. Use your real billable hours—usually 1,000–1,400 a year for a solo—or your rate will be far too low.

Tracking time from memory. Friday-afternoon reconstruction loses billable hours every single week. Log as you go.

Vague time descriptions. "Work — 3 hrs" invites disputes and slow payment. Specific descriptions get invoices paid faster.

Never revisiting the rate. Your costs rise, your skills grow, your demand increases. Recalculate at least once a year and raise your rate accordingly. Charging your year-one rate in year four is a quiet pay cut.

Forgetting to invoice promptly. Tracked hours mean nothing until they're invoiced. Bill on a consistent schedule so cash actually shows up.
