How to Forecast Cash Flow for a Small Business (Without a Finance Degree)

A profitable business can still go broke. It happens all the time: the invoices say you're making money, but the bank account runs dry because a big payment landed late and rent came due first. That gap — between profit on paper and cash in the bank on a specific day — is what kills small businesses.

A cash flow forecast closes that gap. It's a simple projection of the money coming in and going out over the next several weeks or months, so you can see a shortfall before it happens and do something about it. You don't need a finance degree or accounting software. You need a spreadsheet and the steps below.

What a Cash Flow Forecast Actually Is

Strip away the jargon and a cash flow forecast is just this: your expected bank balance, week by week (or month by month), into the future.

You start with the cash you have today. You add the money you expect to come in. You subtract the money you expect to go out. The result is your projected balance at the end of each period. Do that for the next 12 weeks (or 12 months) and you can see, in advance, every week your balance dips dangerously low.

It is not the same as profit. Profit asks "did I earn more than I spent?" Cash flow asks "will I have money in the bank on the day I need it?" Those are different questions, and the second one is what keeps the lights on.

Step 1: Decide Your Time Frame and Periods

For most small businesses, two forecasts are useful:

  • Weekly, 12–13 weeks out — great for tight cash situations and day-to-day survival. This is the classic "13-week cash flow forecast."
  • Monthly, 12 months out — better for bigger-picture planning, budgeting, and decisions like hiring or buying equipment.

If cash is tight, start weekly. If you're stable and planning ahead, start monthly. You can keep both.

Step 2: Start With Your Opening Balance

This is the easiest and most important number: how much cash do you have right now, across your business accounts? That's your opening balance for the first period. Everything else builds from here. Use the real, current bank balance — not what you think you should have.

Step 3: Project Money Coming In (Cash Inflows)

List every source of cash you expect, and crucially, the date you expect it to actually arrive — not the date you invoiced. This is where forecasts go wrong: people record income when it's earned, but cash flow cares only about when the money lands.

Inflows typically include:

  • Customer payments on outstanding invoices (use realistic payment dates — if a client pays in 30 days, put it in the week it'll actually clear).
  • Expected new sales for the period.
  • Any other cash: loans, owner contributions, tax refunds, deposits.

Be conservative. If a client usually pays late, forecast them paying late. An optimistic forecast that's wrong is worse than no forecast — it gives you false confidence.

Step 4: Project Money Going Out (Cash Outflows)

Now list everything leaving the account, again on the date it actually leaves. Two types:

  • Fixed / recurring: rent, payroll, loan repayments, software subscriptions, insurance, utilities. These are predictable — fill them in for every period.
  • Variable / one-off: supplies, inventory, contractor payments, quarterly tax payments, equipment purchases.

Don't forget the lumpy ones that wreck forecasts: quarterly tax bills, annual insurance renewals, and any big planned purchase. Those are exactly the payments that cause a "surprise" shortfall, so put them in their right week.

Step 5: Calculate Your Closing Balance

For each period, the math is simple:

Closing balance = Opening balance + Total inflows − Total outflows

Then the closing balance of one period becomes the opening balance of the next. Chain them together across all your weeks or months and you've got your forecast. In a spreadsheet, this is one formula copied across the row — the closing balance line is the heartbeat of the whole thing.

Step 6: Read the Forecast — Find the Danger Zones

Now look at that closing-balance row across every period. You're hunting for two things:

  • Any period where the balance goes negative or near-zero. That's a cash shortfall — a week you can't pay your bills. Highlight it.
  • The lowest point. Even if you never go negative, knowing your tightest week tells you how much cushion you really have.

A simple conditional-formatting rule that turns any negative or below-threshold balance red gives you an instant visual warning. Now you can act before the crunch, not during it.

Step 7: Fix Shortfalls Before They Arrive

Spotting a shortfall six weeks out gives you options you wouldn't have on the day it hits:

  • Pull cash in sooner: chase outstanding invoices, offer a small early-payment discount, ask for deposits on new work.
  • Push cash out later: negotiate payment terms with suppliers, delay a non-urgent purchase, time a big buy for a stronger week.
  • Bridge the gap: arrange a line of credit while your numbers still look healthy — lenders are far friendlier before a crisis than during one.

This is the entire point of forecasting: turning a future emergency into a present-day, solvable decision.

Step 8: Update It Every Week

A forecast is a living document, not a one-time exercise. Once a week (or month), do two things:

  1. Compare actuals to forecast. What did you predict for last week vs. what actually happened? The gaps teach you to forecast better — maybe you always underestimate how late clients pay.
  2. Roll it forward. Drop the period that just finished, add a new one at the end, and update your opening balance to today's real bank figure.

Fifteen minutes a week keeps your forecast honest and accurate. The first one is always a bit rough; by the third or fourth update it gets genuinely reliable.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Using invoice dates instead of payment dates. Cash flow is about when money moves, not when it's owed.
  • Being too optimistic. Forecast conservatively. Pleasant surprises are fine; nasty ones aren't.
  • Forgetting lumpy payments. Quarterly taxes and annual renewals cause most "surprise" shortfalls.
  • Setting it and forgetting it. A stale forecast is misleading. Update weekly.
  • Confusing it with profit. You can be profitable and still run out of cash. Forecast the cash.

You Now Have Foresight

Build this and you'll always know, weeks in advance, what your bank balance will be and which weeks are tight — turning cash management from anxious guesswork into a calm, planned process.
