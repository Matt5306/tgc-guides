How to Track Marketing ROI: Which Campaigns Actually Make You Money

Learn how to track marketing ROI so you know which campaigns actually make money. A plain-English guide to ROAS, CAC, customer lifetime value, and attribution — with worked examples.

You're spending money on ads, email tools, maybe a freelancer running social. Sales are happening. But ask yourself the uncomfortable question: which of those marketing dollars are actually making you money, and which are just disappearing? Most small business owners can't answer that — they look at total sales, see a number that's not zero, and assume it's working.

Marketing without ROI tracking is just spending and hoping. The owners who grow profitably are the ones who can point to a campaign and say "this one returns $4 for every $1 I put in, and that one loses money." This guide shows you exactly how to figure that out, with the handful of numbers that matter.

What "Marketing ROI" Really Means

ROI — return on investment — is just the profit you made from marketing relative to what you spent on it. The basic formula:

Marketing ROI = (Revenue from Marketing − Marketing Cost) ÷ Marketing Cost

Say you spent $1,000 on a Facebook campaign and it generated $4,000 in sales:

($4,000 − $1,000) ÷ $1,000 = 300% ROI.

Simple enough. The hard part isn't the formula — it's correctly figuring out the two inputs: how much a campaign truly cost, and how much revenue it truly drove. Get those right and the rest is arithmetic.

The Core Metrics You Need to Track

ROAS (Return on Ad Spend)

ROAS is ROI's blunter cousin, used for paid ads. It's just revenue divided by ad spend:

ROAS = Revenue ÷ Ad Spend

The $4,000-from-$1,000 campaign has a ROAS of 4 (often written 4:1 or 400%). A ROAS of 1 means you broke even on revenue — which usually means you lost money once product costs are included. That's the catch with ROAS: it ignores the cost of what you sold. Use it as a quick gauge, then check real ROI.

CAC (Customer Acquisition Cost)

CAC is what it costs to win one new customer:

CAC = Total Marketing Spend ÷ New Customers Acquired

If you spent $2,000 across all marketing in a month and gained 40 new customers:

$2,000 ÷ 40 = $50 CAC.

Every new customer costs you $50 to acquire. Whether that's good or terrible depends entirely on the next number.

Customer Lifetime Value (LTV or CLV)

LTV is the total profit you expect from a customer over the whole time they buy from you — not just the first order.

LTV = Average Order Value × Purchases per Year × Average Customer Lifespan × Gross Margin

Worked example for a coffee subscription:

  • Average order value: $30
  • Purchases per year: 12
  • Average lifespan: 2 years
  • Gross margin: 60%

LTV = $30 × 12 × 2 × 0.60 = $432.

The LTV:CAC Ratio — The Number That Decides Everything

Now compare the two. A customer is worth $432 and costs $50 to acquire:

$432 ÷ $50 = 8.6:1.

That's an excellent ratio. The widely used benchmark is 3:1 — for every $1 you spend acquiring a customer, you want at least $3 of lifetime value back. Below 3:1, your marketing is probably too expensive for your economics. Below 1:1, you're paying to lose customers. This single ratio tells you whether your whole growth model works.

How to Actually Attribute Revenue to Campaigns

Knowing the formulas is useless if you can't tell which campaign drove which sale. This is the part everyone skips, and it's why most ROI tracking is garbage. A few practical methods:

Use unique tracking links (UTMs)

Add UTM tags to every campaign link so your analytics can tell where a visitor came from. A link like yourstore.com/?utm_source=facebook&utm_campaign=spring_sale lets you see exactly how many sales the spring sale campaign on Facebook produced. Set these up consistently and your analytics does the attribution for you.

Use discount/promo codes per channel

Give each channel its own code — INSTA10 for Instagram, EMAIL10 for your newsletter. When the code is used at checkout, you know which channel earned the sale. Crude but effective, and it works even when link tracking breaks down.

Just ask

"How did you hear about us?" at checkout or in onboarding is unglamorous but surprisingly accurate for word-of-mouth and offline channels that UTMs miss entirely.

A Full Worked Comparison

Here's the whole point of tracking — putting campaigns side by side:

Campaign Spend Revenue New Customers CAC ROAS
Facebook Ads $1,000 $4,000 30 $33 4.0
Google Ads $1,500 $3,000 20 $75 2.0
Email $200 $2,500 15 $13 12.5
Influencer $800 $900 6 $133 1.1

Now the decisions write themselves. Email is your hidden gem — a ROAS of 12.5 and a $13 CAC; pour more here. Facebook is solid. Google is borderline — okay ROAS but a high CAC, worth optimizing. The influencer campaign is losing money — a ROAS of 1.1 means once product costs come out, you're underwater. Cut it or renegotiate. Without this table, you'd see "$10,400 in total sales, great!" and never know one channel was bleeding you.

Common Marketing ROI Mistakes

  • Tracking revenue, not profit. A ROAS of 2 sounds positive, but if your gross margin is 40%, that $2 of revenue is only 80 cents of gross profit against $1 of spend — you lost money. Always factor in margin before celebrating.
  • Ignoring lifetime value. Judging a campaign on first-purchase revenue alone makes acquisition look unaffordable. A $50 CAC looks bad against a $30 first order but great against a $432 LTV. Use the full picture.
  • No attribution system. If you can't tell which campaign drove a sale, every ROI number is a guess. Set up UTMs and promo codes before you spend, not after.
  • Forgetting the hidden costs. Marketing cost isn't just ad spend. Include software, the freelancer's fee, and your own time. The Instagram campaign that's "free" still costs the ten hours a week you spend on it.
  • Killing campaigns too early. Some channels (SEO, email list building) pay back over months, not days. Don't axe a long-game channel using a short-game yardstick — match the measurement window to the channel.
  • Chasing ROAS while shrinking. You can hit an amazing ROAS by spending almost nothing — but tiny spend means tiny growth. Balance efficiency with scale.

Find Your Winners and Losers This Month

Pull your last three months of marketing spend and lay every campaign in a table like the one above: spend, revenue, new customers, CAC, ROAS. Then calculate your LTV once and check each channel against the 3:1 benchmark. You will almost certainly find one campaign quietly losing money and one quietly outperforming — and reallocating between them is the fastest profit win in marketing.
