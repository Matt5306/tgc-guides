How to Read a Profit and Loss Statement (Small Business Owner's Guide)

Learn how to read a profit and loss statement step by step. A plain-English guide for small business owners covering revenue, COGS, gross profit, operating expenses, and net profit — with a worked example.

Your accountant hands you a profit and loss statement, or your bookkeeping software spits one out, and your eyes glaze over. Rows of numbers, a few subtotals, and somewhere down the bottom a figure that's supposed to tell you whether the business is winning or losing. If you've ever nodded along in a meeting while having no real idea what those numbers mean, this guide is for you.

A profit and loss statement (also called a P&L or income statement) is just a story about your money over a period of time — usually a month, quarter, or year. It tells you how much came in, how much went out, and what was left. Once you understand the four or five building blocks, you'll never feel lost looking at one again.

What a P&L Actually Shows

A P&L covers a period, not a moment. That's the key difference between it and a balance sheet. A balance sheet is a snapshot of what you own and owe on a single day. A P&L is a movie of what happened between two dates: "January 1 to January 31" or "all of 2025."

It answers one question in increasing levels of detail: Did I make money, and where did it go?

Every P&L works top to bottom. You start with the money customers paid you, then subtract costs in layers. Each subtraction gives you a new subtotal that tells you something specific. Read it like a waterfall.

The Building Blocks, Top to Bottom

1. Revenue (a.k.a. Sales or Top Line)

This is the total money you earned from selling your product or service before any costs come out. If you run a coffee shop and sold $40,000 worth of coffee and pastries in January, your revenue is $40,000.

A trap to watch: revenue is what you earned, not necessarily what hit your bank account. If you invoiced a client $5,000 in January but they pay in February, that $5,000 still counts as January revenue under accrual accounting. This catches a lot of owners off guard.

2. Cost of Goods Sold (COGS)

COGS is the direct cost of making or buying the thing you sold. For the coffee shop, that's coffee beans, milk, cups, and pastry ingredients. It does not include rent, your salary, or marketing — those come later.

Say the beans, milk, and ingredients for January cost $12,000. That's your COGS.

The rule of thumb: if you sold zero units, would this cost disappear? If yes, it's probably COGS. Rent doesn't disappear when sales drop, so it's not COGS. Bean costs do, so they are.

3. Gross Profit

Now the first real insight:

Gross Profit = Revenue − COGS

For the coffee shop: $40,000 − $12,000 = $28,000 gross profit.

Gross profit tells you how much money is left to run the rest of the business after the direct cost of your product. Expressed as a percentage, this is your gross margin:

$28,000 ÷ $40,000 = 70% gross margin.

That number is gold. It tells you that for every dollar of coffee sold, 70 cents is available to cover everything else. If your gross margin is shrinking month over month, your pricing or your supplier costs are drifting in the wrong direction, and you want to catch that early.

4. Operating Expenses

These are the costs of running the business that aren't tied directly to each sale. Often grouped and sometimes called SG&A (Selling, General & Administrative). Common ones:

  • Rent — $4,000
  • Wages for staff (not direct production) — $9,000
  • Utilities — $800
  • Marketing — $1,500
  • Software and subscriptions — $400
  • Insurance — $300

Total operating expenses for the coffee shop: $16,000.

5. Operating Profit

Operating Profit = Gross Profit − Operating Expenses

$28,000 − $16,000 = $12,000 operating profit.

This is one of the most important numbers on the whole statement. It shows whether the core business — selling coffee and running the shop — actually makes money before you account for things like loan interest and taxes. A business can have great revenue and still post a thin or negative operating profit if expenses are out of control.

6. Net Profit (the Bottom Line)

Finally, subtract the leftovers: interest on any loans and taxes.

  • Operating profit: $12,000
  • Loan interest: −$700
  • Taxes: −$2,800

Net Profit = $8,500.

This is "the bottom line" — literally the last line. It's what the business actually kept for the period.

A Full Worked Example

Here's the whole coffee shop P&L for January in one place:

Line Amount
Revenue $40,000
COGS −$12,000
Gross Profit $28,000
Operating Expenses −$16,000
Operating Profit $12,000
Interest −$700
Taxes −$2,800
Net Profit $8,500

Read top to bottom, each subtotal answers a question: How much came in? ($40k) What did the product cost? ($12k) What's left to run on? ($28k) What does running it cost? ($16k) Does the core business work? (Yes, $12k) What did we actually keep? ($8,500).

The Ratios That Make It Useful

Raw dollars are fine, but percentages let you compare months and spot trends:

  • Gross margin = Gross Profit ÷ Revenue → 70% here.
  • Operating margin = Operating Profit ÷ Revenue → $12,000 ÷ $40,000 = 30%.
  • Net margin = Net Profit ÷ Revenue → $8,500 ÷ $40,000 = 21%.

Track these three every month. If gross margin holds but operating margin falls, your overhead is creeping up. If gross margin itself drops, look at pricing or supplier costs. The percentages tell you where the problem lives.

Common Mistakes When Reading a P&L

  • Confusing profit with cash. A P&L can show $8,500 net profit while your bank account is shrinking, because of timing (unpaid invoices), loan principal payments, or buying inventory. Profit is not the same as cash flow. Always look at both.
  • Putting overhead into COGS (or vice versa). Misclassifying rent as COGS will distort your gross margin and make month-to-month comparisons meaningless. Keep the categories consistent.
  • Only looking at the bottom line. Net profit is the headline, but the middle is where you fix things. A bad month usually shows up as a falling gross margin or a spike in one operating expense — invisible if you only glance at the final number.
  • Ignoring the trend. A single month tells you almost nothing. The value comes from comparing this month to last month and the same month last year. Patterns reveal problems; snapshots hide them.
  • Forgetting your own salary. Many owners don't pay themselves a proper wage, which makes the business look more profitable than it is. Build a realistic owner's wage into operating expenses so you see the true picture.

Start Reading Yours This Month

Pull your most recent P&L and walk down it line by line using the order above: revenue, COGS, gross profit, operating expenses, operating profit, net profit. Calculate your three margins. You'll likely spot one number that surprises you — and that surprise is usually where the money is leaking.
