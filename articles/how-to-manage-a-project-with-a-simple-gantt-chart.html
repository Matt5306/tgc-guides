How to Manage a Project with a Simple Gantt Chart

Learn how to manage any project with a simple Gantt chart—plan tasks, set realistic deadlines, track dependencies, and keep your team on schedule without expensive software.

You've got a project with a dozen moving parts, a deadline that feels too close, and a sinking feeling that something important is going to slip through the cracks. Maybe it's a website launch, an office move, a product release, or a home renovation. The tasks are clear enough in your head, but the moment you try to figure out what has to happen when—and what depends on what—everything turns to mush.

A Gantt chart fixes that. It's a horizontal bar chart that lays out every task against a timeline, so you can see at a glance what's happening now, what's coming next, and where the bottlenecks are. You don't need a project management degree or a $30/month software subscription. You need a clear list of tasks and a grid. This guide walks you through building and managing a project with a simple Gantt chart, step by step.

What a Gantt Chart Actually Shows You

A Gantt chart has three core pieces:

  • Tasks listed down the left side, in roughly the order they'll happen.
  • A timeline across the top—usually days or weeks.
  • Bars that stretch across the timeline showing when each task starts and ends.

The magic is in seeing all of it together. A task list tells you what needs doing. A Gantt chart tells you when, how long, and what's blocking what. When two bars overlap, you know those tasks run at the same time. When one bar ends right where another begins, you've got a dependency—the second task can't start until the first finishes.

That visual layout is what turns a vague to-do list into an actual plan.

Step 1: Break the Project Into Tasks

Before you touch a timeline, dump every task out of your head. Don't worry about order yet—just get them down. For a small website launch, your list might look like this:

  • Finalize site content
  • Design homepage mockup
  • Build homepage
  • Build inner pages
  • Set up hosting
  • Test on mobile
  • Launch

Aim for tasks that take somewhere between a half-day and a week. If a task is bigger than a week, break it into smaller chunks—"Build site" is too vague to schedule, but "Build homepage" and "Build inner pages" are concrete enough to assign dates to.

Step 2: Estimate Duration for Each Task

Now put a realistic length on each task. Here's where most people get burned: they estimate the ideal time, not the real time. "Design homepage mockup" might take four focused hours, but spread across a week of meetings, emails, and revisions, the calendar duration is three days. Estimate calendar days, not heads-down hours.

Task Duration
Finalize site content 3 days
Design homepage mockup 3 days
Build homepage 4 days
Build inner pages 5 days
Set up hosting 1 day
Test on mobile 2 days
Launch 1 day

When in doubt, add a buffer. A task you think is two days is probably three.

Step 3: Map the Dependencies

This is the step that separates a real plan from wishful thinking. Ask of every task: What has to be done before I can start this?

  • "Build homepage" can't start until "Design homepage mockup" is done.
  • "Test on mobile" can't start until the pages are built.
  • "Launch" can't happen until testing passes and hosting is live.

Some tasks have no dependencies and can run in parallel. "Set up hosting" doesn't need to wait for design—you can do it any time before launch. Spotting those parallel tasks is how you compress a timeline. If three things can happen at once, your project doesn't take as long as the sum of its parts.

Step 4: Lay It Out on the Timeline

Now place the bars. Start with your first task on day one, then chain dependent tasks so each begins when its predecessor ends. Run independent tasks in parallel where you can.

Here's the worked example. Say the project starts Monday, June 1:

  • Finalize content: June 1–3
  • Design mockup: June 1–3 (runs parallel—content and design don't block each other)
  • Build homepage: June 4–9 (waits for mockup)
  • Build inner pages: June 10–16 (waits for homepage, reuses the layout)
  • Set up hosting: June 4 (one day, slotted in early since nothing blocks it)
  • Test on mobile: June 17–18 (waits for all pages)
  • Launch: June 19

Reading the chart, you immediately see the critical path—the chain of dependent tasks that determines the finish date: content/design → homepage → inner pages → testing → launch. Anything that slips on that path pushes the whole launch. Hosting, sitting off to the side with slack, can slip a week and nobody notices.

Step 5: Track Progress and Adjust

A Gantt chart isn't a one-time document—it's a living dashboard. Each week, update it:

  • Shade completed portions of each bar so you can see what's done versus planned.
  • Move bars when reality diverges from the plan. If "Build homepage" runs two days late, push everything downstream and look at what it does to your launch date.
  • Watch the critical path. A delay on a non-critical task is annoying; a delay on a critical-path task is the difference between launching on time and not.

The whole point is early warning. When you can see that a slip on Tuesday threatens a launch three weeks out, you can react—reassign help, cut scope, or move the date deliberately instead of discovering the problem the day before go-live.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Listing tasks too big. "Do the marketing" isn't a task—it's a project of its own. If you can't put a clear start and end on a bar, break it down.

Ignoring dependencies. Scheduling tasks back-to-back without asking what blocks what produces a beautiful chart that's pure fiction. Map the dependencies before you place a single bar.

No buffer time. Real projects have sick days, revisions, and "wait, the client wants changes." A plan with zero slack breaks the first time anything goes sideways. Build in breathing room, especially before hard deadlines.

Never updating it. A Gantt chart you build once and never touch is wallpaper. The value comes from revisiting it weekly and adjusting as reality unfolds.

Overcomplicating it. You don't need 80 tasks, color-coded resource leveling, and percent-complete tracking on a five-week project. For small projects, a clean grid with 10–20 tasks does everything you need.
