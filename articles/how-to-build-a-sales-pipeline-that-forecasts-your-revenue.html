How to Build a Sales Pipeline That Forecasts Your Revenue

Learn how to build a sales pipeline that actually forecasts revenue. A plain-English guide to pipeline stages, weighted forecasting, conversion rates, and velocity — with worked examples for small businesses.

If someone asked you right now how much revenue you'll close next month, could you give a number you'd bet on? Most small business owners can't. They have a vague sense — "a few deals are looking good" — and then the month ends and reality is either a pleasant surprise or a gut punch. Either way, you were guessing.

A sales pipeline fixes that. It's a structured way to track every potential deal through predictable stages, and when you set it up right, it turns "I think things are okay" into "I'll close roughly $42,000 next month." This guide shows you how to build one that doesn't just organize your deals — it forecasts your revenue.

What a Sales Pipeline Actually Is

A pipeline is the journey every prospect takes from "never heard of you" to "paying customer," broken into stages. Each deal sits in exactly one stage at a time and moves forward (or drops out) as things progress.

Think of it as a series of buckets. New leads pour into the first bucket. Some advance to the next, some leak out. By the last bucket, you have closed deals. The shape of how deals flow through those buckets is what lets you predict the future — because the pattern repeats.

Step 1: Define Your Pipeline Stages

Your stages should map to your actual sales process, but a clean starting point for most small businesses is five stages:

  1. Lead — A prospect who's entered your world but you haven't qualified yet.
  2. Qualified — You've confirmed they have a real need, budget, and authority to buy.
  3. Proposal — You've sent a quote or proposal.
  4. Negotiation — They're engaged, working out terms or price.
  5. Closed — Won (they bought) or Lost (they didn't).

The exact names matter less than two rules: every stage must represent a clear, observable step, and a deal can only be in one stage at a time. Vague stages like "interested" are useless because you can't tell when a deal qualifies to move. "Sent a proposal" is observable — either you did or you didn't.

Step 2: Track the Right Data Per Deal

For every deal in your pipeline, record at minimum:

  • Deal name / company
  • Deal value (the dollar amount if it closes)
  • Current stage
  • Expected close date
  • Date entered the pipeline (for velocity later)

That's enough to forecast. A simple snapshot:

Deal Value Stage Expected Close
Acme Co $20,000 Proposal June 15
Bright LLC $8,000 Qualified June 30
Nova Inc $50,000 Negotiation June 20
Peak Ltd $5,000 Lead July 10

Step 3: Calculate Conversion Rates Between Stages

Here's where a pipeline becomes a forecasting tool. Look at your history: of all the deals that reached each stage, what percentage moved to the next one?

Suppose over the last year you saw:

  • Lead → Qualified: 50%
  • Qualified → Proposal: 60%
  • Proposal → Negotiation: 70%
  • Negotiation → Closed Won: 80%

Multiply them to get the overall close rate from a fresh lead:

0.50 × 0.60 × 0.70 × 0.80 = 0.168, or about 17%.

So roughly 1 in 6 raw leads becomes a customer. Now you know something powerful: if you need 10 new customers, you need about 60 qualified leads, or even more raw ones at the top. Conversion rates turn revenue goals into concrete lead targets.

Step 4: Build a Weighted Forecast

This is the heart of pipeline forecasting. Instead of assuming every open deal will close (wildly optimistic) or ignoring them (useless), you weight each deal by the probability it closes from its current stage.

Assign a probability to each stage based on your conversion history:

  • Lead: 10%
  • Qualified: 30%
  • Proposal: 50%
  • Negotiation: 80%

Now multiply each deal's value by its stage probability:

Deal Value Stage Probability Weighted Value
Acme Co $20,000 Proposal 50% $10,000
Bright LLC $8,000 Qualified 30% $2,400
Nova Inc $50,000 Negotiation 80% $40,000
Peak Ltd $5,000 Lead 10% $500

Total weighted pipeline = $52,900.

That's your forecast. Not the $83,000 of raw pipeline value (which assumes everything closes), and not zero — but a probability-adjusted $52,900 you can actually plan around. Run this every week and you'll watch the number firm up as deals advance and their probabilities climb.

Step 5: Track Pipeline Velocity

Velocity tells you how fast money moves through your pipeline — and it bundles your key health metrics into one number:

Pipeline Velocity = (Number of Deals × Average Deal Value × Win Rate) ÷ Sales Cycle Length (days)

Worked example:

  • Open deals: 20
  • Average deal value: $10,000
  • Win rate: 17%
  • Average sales cycle: 30 days

Velocity = (20 × $10,000 × 0.17) ÷ 30 = $1,133 per day.

You're generating about $1,133 of closed revenue per day from your pipeline. The value of this number is that it shows you the four levers of growth: more deals, bigger deals, a higher win rate, or a shorter cycle. Improve any one and revenue rises. It tells you where to focus, not just that you should "sell more."

Common Sales Pipeline Mistakes

  • Counting raw pipeline value as the forecast. Treating $83,000 of open deals as $83,000 of expected revenue is how owners overspend and then panic. Always weight by stage probability.
  • Letting deals rot in a stage. A deal stuck in "Proposal" for three months isn't a live deal — it's a ghost inflating your forecast. Set a maximum age per stage and either advance or kill stale deals.
  • Vague, unobservable stages. If you can't objectively say whether a deal belongs in a stage, your conversion rates are noise. Define stages by concrete actions.
  • Guessing probabilities instead of measuring. Stage probabilities should come from your actual historical conversion rates, not optimism. If only 40% of negotiations close, don't weight them at 80%.
  • Ignoring the top of the funnel. A healthy bottom of the pipeline today means nothing if no new leads are entering. Watch your lead inflow as closely as your closing.
  • Forecasting once a month. Pipelines change daily. A forecast you update weekly catches problems — like a dry top of funnel — while there's still time to react.

Build Yours Before Next Month Closes

Start by writing down your five stages and listing every open deal with its value, stage, and expected close date. Assign a probability to each stage, multiply, and total it — that's your first weighted forecast. Even a rough version beats guessing, and it gets sharper every week as you refine your conversion rates with real data.
