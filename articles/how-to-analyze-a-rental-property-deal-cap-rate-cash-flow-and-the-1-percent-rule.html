How to Analyze a Rental Property Deal: Cap Rate, Cash Flow & the 1% Rule

You've found a rental property that looks promising. The listing says it rents for $1,800 a month and the price is $240,000. Good deal? Bad deal? The honest answer is: you can't tell yet — not from the asking price and rent alone. A property that looks great on the surface can lose money every month once you account for the mortgage, taxes, insurance, repairs, and the months it sits empty.

This guide gives you the four numbers that actually tell you whether a rental deal works: the 1% rule, cap rate, cash flow, and cash-on-cash return. We'll run a real example all the way through so you can do this on any property you look at.

Step 1: The 1% Rule (A 10-Second Filter)

The 1% rule is a quick screen, not a verdict. It says:

Monthly rent should be at least 1% of the purchase price.

For a $240,000 property, 1% is $2,400/month. Our example rents for $1,800. That's 0.75% — below the rule. This doesn't automatically kill the deal, but it's a yellow flag telling you to dig deeper before you fall in love.

Use the 1% rule to decide which properties are worth analyzing fully. In expensive coastal markets, almost nothing hits 1%, so investors there accept lower ratios and lean on appreciation. In the Midwest and South, hitting or beating 1% is realistic. Know your market's normal.

Step 2: Calculate Real Monthly Cash Flow

Cash flow is the money left in your pocket each month after every expense, including the mortgage. This is the number that determines whether the property feeds you or you feed it.

Start with income, then subtract expenses. The mistake beginners make is forgetting the expenses that don't show up every month — vacancy, repairs, and capital expenditures (big-ticket replacements like a roof or furnace).

The income side

  • Monthly rent: $1,800
  • (Optional) other income — laundry, parking, pet rent: $0 for now

The expense side

Let's assume you put 20% down ($48,000) on the $240,000 property and finance $192,000 at 7% over 30 years.

Expense Monthly amount How to estimate it
Mortgage (principal + interest) $1,277 Loan calculator on $192,000 at 7%, 30 yrs
Property taxes $250 Annual tax bill ÷ 12
Insurance $100 Landlord policy quote ÷ 12
Vacancy reserve $90 5% of rent
Repairs/maintenance $135 7.5% of rent
Capital expenditures (CapEx) $135 7.5% of rent
Property management $144 8% of rent (even if you self-manage, count it)
Total expenses $2,131

Cash flow = $1,800 − $2,131 = −$331 per month.

This property loses $331 every month. The asking price and rent looked fine, but the full math says you'd pay almost $4,000 a year for the privilege of owning it. That's exactly the kind of deal the 1% rule warned you about.

A note on vacancy, repairs, and CapEx: skipping these is the single most common way new investors fool themselves into a bad deal. Tenants move out. Water heaters die. Roofs need replacing. If you don't reserve for it monthly, the bill arrives all at once and erases a year of "profit." Always budget for them even when the property is currently rented and quiet.

Step 3: Calculate the Cap Rate

Cap rate (capitalization rate) measures the property's return independent of how you finance it. It's how investors compare two properties apples-to-apples. The formula:

Cap Rate = Net Operating Income (NOI) ÷ Purchase Price

NOI is your annual income minus annual operating expenses but excluding the mortgage. Financing is a choice; the cap rate measures the asset itself.

Using our numbers, operating expenses (everything except the $1,277 mortgage) are $854/month, or $10,248/year. Annual rent is $21,600.

  • NOI = $21,600 − $10,248 = $11,352/year
  • Cap Rate = $11,352 ÷ $240,000 = 4.7%

How to read it:

  • 4–5% cap rate: typical for lower-risk, higher-cost markets; thin for cash flow.
  • 6–8% cap rate: a healthier range for buy-and-hold cash-flow investors.
  • 9%+ cap rate: often signals higher risk, a rough neighborhood, or a property needing work — verify why it's so high.

Our 4.7% confirms what cash flow already told us: as a pure cash-flow play, this is a weak deal at this price.

Step 4: Cash-on-Cash Return (The One Most People Skip)

Cash-on-cash return answers the most personal question: what return am I getting on the actual cash I put in? This is the number to compare against just leaving your money in an index fund.

Cash-on-Cash = Annual Cash Flow ÷ Total Cash Invested

Total cash invested isn't just the down payment. Include closing costs and any upfront repairs:

  • Down payment: $48,000
  • Closing costs (~3%): $7,200
  • Initial repairs/turnover: $3,000
  • Total cash in: $58,200

Annual cash flow here is negative (−$331 × 12 = −$3,972), so cash-on-cash is −6.8%. You'd be paying to invest. Most investors target a cash-on-cash return of 8% or higher before a deal is worth the effort and risk.

How to Turn a Bad Deal Into a Good One

The analysis isn't just pass/fail — it tells you what would have to change. For this property to hit break-even cash flow, you'd need one of:

  • A lower price. Offer around $200,000 instead of $240,000 and the mortgage drops enough to get close to neutral.
  • Higher rent. If the market actually supports $2,150+, the picture flips positive.
  • More money down. Putting 30–40% down cuts the mortgage but also lowers your cash-on-cash return — you're just trading one problem for another.
  • Lower expenses. Self-managing saves the 8% management fee, but don't pretend your time is free.

Run the numbers at your offer price, not the asking price. The whole point of analysis is to tell you the maximum you can pay and still make money — then you negotiate toward it.

A Repeatable Checklist for Every Property

  1. Apply the 1% rule as a quick screen.
  2. Build the full expense list, including vacancy, repairs, CapEx, and management — never just the mortgage.
  3. Calculate monthly cash flow. Negative is a hard stop unless you're explicitly betting on appreciation.
  4. Calculate cap rate to compare the asset to others.
  5. Calculate cash-on-cash return to compare against other uses of your money.
  6. Find your max offer price by working backward from the cash flow and return you require.

Do this on every property and you'll quickly develop a feel for your market — and you'll avoid the expensive lesson of buying a "great deal" that bleeds money every month.
